## Summary

The international community and international legal scholars evaluate the Arunachal Pradesh territorial dispute through the prism of treaty history, international boundary demarcation law, and regional geopolitical stability. The disputed boundary was negotiated at the 1914 Simla Conference between plenipotentiaries representing British India (Sir Henry McMahon) and the Tibetan government (Lonchen Shatra), establishing the 890-kilometer McMahon Line along the Himalayan crest as the formal boundary between British India and Tibet. While Chinese republican representative Ivan Chen initialed the main convention map, the central Chinese government in Beijing declined final ratification, disputing the internal boundary between Outer and Inner Tibet. Today, the United States explicitly recognizes Arunachal Pradesh as an integral part of the Republic of India and recognizes the McMahon Line as the international boundary, formally condemning any unilateral Chinese military or civilian incursions across the LAC. Neutral international policy institutes emphasize that India's sustained, effective administrative, constitutional, and electoral jurisdiction over the region establishes an incontrovertible sovereign status under customary international law.

## Developments

### 2026-07-10

The US Department of State Principal Deputy Spokesperson reaffirmed during a press briefing that the United States recognizes the state of Arunachal Pradesh as Indian territory and strongly opposes any unilateral attempts by China to advance territorial claims through incursions or geographic renaming.

### 2024-03-20

The US Senate Foreign Relations Committee reaffirmed bipartisan Senate Resolution 175, titled "Reaffirming the State of Arunachal Pradesh as Indian Territory and Condemning the People's Republic of China's Provocations," commending India's defense of its sovereign borders.

### 2023-11-15

The Lowy Institute published a comprehensive international legal analysis of the 1914 Simla Convention and the McMahon Line, demonstrating that Tibetan treaty-making authority between 1912 and 1950 satisfied the criteria for bilateral international agreements, creating binding border demarcations under the principle of state succession.
